Glossary

Master Control Room (MCR)
The technical hub of a TV channel, where programme sources are switched, monitored and sent to transmission, staffed around the clock.
Playout
The system that plays a channel's scheduled programmes, adverts and graphics to air, following the playlist.
Transmission chain
The path a channel's signal takes after playout: encoding, multiplexing, modulation and uplink or distribution to viewers.
Dead air
An unintended black screen or silence on a broadcast channel, the failure every MCR is built to prevent.
SDI (Serial Digital Interface)
The coaxial-cable standard for carrying uncompressed digital video and audio between broadcast equipment.
SMPTE ST 2110
A suite of standards for carrying video, audio and data as separate streams over managed IP networks instead of SDI cables.
PTP (Precision Time Protocol)
The timing protocol (IEEE 1588, profiled for broadcast in SMPTE ST 2059) that keeps every device in an IP media facility in sync.
N+1 redundancy
One standby unit that can take over from any one of N active units, cheaper than duplicating everything.
2N redundancy
A complete duplicate of every component, so the whole primary path can fail and a second path carries on.
Failover
Switching from a failed primary path to its backup, automatically or by an operator, ideally without viewers noticing.
Encoder
A device or software that compresses video and audio (for example H.264 or HEVC) for satellite, cable or streaming distribution.
Uplink
Sending the channel's signal from an earth station to a satellite, which relays it to viewers and cable operators.
Multiviewer
A wall display that shows many video sources at once, with audio meters and alarms, so operators can watch everything in one view.
Shift handover
The structured transfer of status, open issues and upcoming events from one operator to the next at a shift change.
Rec.709
The ITU colour standard for HD television, which sets the colours and levels that are safe for broadcast.

Questions

What does a broadcast engineer do in a master control room?

A broadcast engineer in the MCR keeps the channel on air: watching every source and the outgoing signal, switching to backups when something fails, running scheduled live events, maintaining playout, encoding and transmission equipment, and logging every incident so it does not happen again.

What is the difference between N+1 and 2N redundancy in broadcasting?

N+1 keeps one spare unit that can replace any single failed unit among N, which is affordable but covers only one failure at a time. 2N duplicates the entire chain, so a whole path can fail and the second one carries on, at roughly twice the cost. Many TV channels mix both: 2N for playout and transmission, N+1 elsewhere.

What is SMPTE ST 2110 and why are TV channels moving to it?

SMPTE ST 2110 sends video, audio and data as separate streams over IP networks, timed by PTP. Channels move to it for flexibility, scalability and remote production, but it needs careful network design, timing and staff training, so most stations migrate in phases rather than all at once.

How do TV channels prevent dead air?

By layering defences: redundant playout and transmission paths, automatic failover, constant monitoring with clear alarms, tested backup content, preventive maintenance, written procedures and disciplined shift handovers, plus a practised response for the first seconds when something still goes wrong.
