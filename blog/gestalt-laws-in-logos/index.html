Gestalt in the Wild: 7 Laws Hiding in Every Logo You Know

You already see proximity, closure and figure/ground every day. You just don't have the names. Seven Gestalt laws, one example each, and a 10-second self-test to train your eye.

Abdullah Al Fahim28 Nov 2026 · 3 min read

After reading this, you’ll be able to name the seven Gestalt laws you see in logos every day, and use them on purpose in your own work.

Photo by sergiovisor_ph on Pixabay (Pixabay Content License).

Why it matters

Gestalt psychology, developed in Germany in the early 20th century by researchers such as Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler and Kurt Koffka, describes how our brains organise what we see into groups and wholes. Designers use it constantly, often without naming it. Once you can name the laws, you can diagnose why a layout feels messy and design marks that people read instantly.

I introduced Gestalt briefly in Design theory you can use tomorrow. This post goes law by law, with logos.

The idea explained

  1. Proximity. Things close together look like a group. Example: the separated stripes in IBM’s logo still read as letters because each letter’s stripes sit close together.
  2. Similarity. Things that look alike (colour, shape, size) seem related. Example: the repeated coloured petals of NBC’s peacock read as one bird.
  3. Closure. We complete incomplete shapes. Example: the WWF panda has no full outline, yet we see the whole animal.
  4. Continuity. The eye follows smooth lines and curves. Example: the Coca-Cola script flows as one continuous movement.
  5. Figure/ground. We separate an object from its background, and sometimes the background becomes a shape. Example: the arrow in FedEx’s negative space, covered in Logos with a second meaning.
  6. Common region. Things inside the same boundary are grouped. Example: badge and emblem logos, like Starbucks’ circular frame.
  7. Symmetry and order (Prägnanz). We prefer the simplest, most stable interpretation. Example: the three overlapping ovals of Toyota’s emblem read as one balanced shape.

Try it yourself: the 10-second self-test

Look at the last poster, app screen or slide you designed:

  1. Which elements belong together? Are they closer to each other than to anything else?
  2. Do related items look alike?
  3. Is there a shape your eye completes or follows?
  4. Is the figure clearly separated from the ground?
  5. Is anything accidentally grouped because it sits in the same box or colour?

If one answer surprises you, adjust spacing first. It’s usually the fastest fix.

Common mistakes

  • Equal spacing everywhere. If everything is equally far apart, nothing is grouped.
  • Colour without meaning. Random colours create false similarity groups.
  • Too much closure. Leave out too much and people can’t complete the shape.
  • Ignoring the ground. Accidental shapes in negative space are easy to miss.
  • Boxes around everything. Common region is powerful; too many boxes become clutter.

Summary

  • Gestalt laws describe how people group what they see.
  • Proximity and similarity are the most useful in everyday layouts.
  • Closure, continuity and figure/ground make logos memorable.
  • Common region and symmetry create order and stability.
  • When a layout feels messy, fix spacing first.
