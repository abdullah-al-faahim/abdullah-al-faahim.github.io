Tanvir and the 50/50 Myth: What Work-Life Balance Really Means

Tanvir works night shifts at a TV channel in Tejgaon and has decided work-life balance is impossible for people like him. Then his grandmother, peeling oranges on a winter afternoon, gives him a better definition. A Friday story.

A Friday story. The people and places are fictional; the lessons are real.

Tanvir had read the articles. Eight hours of work, eight hours of rest, eight hours of life. He laughed every time.

Tanvir Hasan was 30, a broadcast engineer in the master control room of a TV channel in Tejgaon. His roster rotated: mornings one week, evenings the next, nights after that. Live cricket ran over. Breaking news arrived without asking. Eid meant working while the city celebrated.

“Work-life balance,” he told his cousin Arif over the phone, “is for people with normal jobs.”

The guilt

He felt it everywhere. When he missed his nephew’s birthday because of a night shift, he felt guilty about family. When he left the control room on time and a problem appeared an hour later, he felt guilty about work. When he finally had a day off, he spent it asleep, and felt guilty about that too.

Every day was supposed to be balanced. Not one of them was.

Nani’s oranges

One Friday in late November, Tanvir visited his grandmother in Mohammadpur. Nani sat on the veranda in a shawl, peeling oranges, the radio playing old songs.

“You look tired, bhai,” she said.

He told her about the shifts, the guilt, the articles with their perfect eight-eight-eight.

Nani laughed and handed him half an orange. “When your grandfather was building his business, he worked until midnight for two years. When your mother was born, he came home every day for lunch. When I was ill, he closed the shop for a month. Was he balanced?”

“Some years, no,” Tanvir said.

“Over his life? Yes.” She wiped her fingers. “You are measuring the wrong thing. You measure a day. Measure a season.”

A better definition

On the bus home, Tanvir wrote in his phone’s notes:

Control. Some say over when and how I work. He couldn’t choose his shifts, but he could swap with colleagues in advance and plan around the roster.

Recovery. Enough rest that he started each roster week with energy, not exhaustion.

Presence. When he was at work, fully there. When at home, actually home, not half-reading the control room WhatsApp group.

Priorities. Time going to the things he said mattered: his parents, his health, the guitar he hadn’t touched in a year.

Hours weren’t on the list.

The scorecard

That night, he scored himself from 1 to 5 on each.

Control: 3. Recovery: 1. Presence: 2. Priorities: 2.

Recovery was the problem. He’d been treating days off as “catch-up sleep and nothing else”, and he was never really rested.

So he changed one thing. After each night-shift week, he protected the first full day off: sleep until late morning, then a walk by the lake at Dhanmondi, lunch with his parents, and no work messages until the evening.

Three months later

It wasn’t perfect. Some weeks were still brutal. But on the Friday he visited Nani again, he brought his guitar.

“Balanced?” she asked.

“This season,” he said, “more than before.”

Try this week

Score yourself from 1 to 5 on control, recovery, presence and priorities. Pick the lowest score and make one small change, like protecting one evening, one morning or one full day off.

Summary

  • Balance isn’t a daily 50/50 split; measure it over weeks and seasons.
  • Real balance means control, recovery, presence and priorities.
  • Hours matter less than whether you recover.
  • Balance changes with your life stage.
  • Score yourself on the four parts and fix the lowest first.
