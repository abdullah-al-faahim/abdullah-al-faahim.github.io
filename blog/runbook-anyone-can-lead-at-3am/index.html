Writing the Runbook So Anyone Can Lead at 3 AM

A runbook is leadership you leave behind. Written well, it lets the most junior person on the night shift handle a fault the way your best engineer would. Here is how to write one people actually use.

At 3 a.m., the most experienced engineer in the building is usually asleep at home. Whoever is on shift has to deal with whatever happens, often alone, often tired, sometimes new to the job.

That’s why I think a good runbook is one of the most powerful leadership tools a technical team has. It is your best judgement, written down, available to anyone at any hour. When it’s done well, the night shift doesn’t need you awake. When it’s done badly, they call you, or worse, they guess.

Photo: Building Architecture by Vladimir Kudinov, CC0, via StockSnap.

What a runbook is (and isn’t)

A runbook is a set of step-by-step instructions for specific situations: a feed loss, an encoder failure, a power changeover, a satellite issue, a scheduled maintenance task.

It is not:

  • A manual that explains how the equipment works.
  • A policy document full of “should” and “ensure”.
  • A diary of what happened last time.

It answers one question: “This is happening. What do I do, in what order?”

Write for the tired, new person

The test I use: could someone in their first month, at 3 a.m., under pressure, follow this without calling anyone? That changes how you write:

  • Short numbered steps, one action each.
  • Exact names: the label on the panel, the name on the screen, the button text. Not “switch to the backup source” but “On router panel 2, press BKP-1”.
  • What you should see next: “The tally light on monitor 4 turns red.”
  • What to do if you don’t: “If the picture doesn’t return within 10 seconds, go to step 6.”

Vague steps feel efficient to the writer and impossible to the reader.

Start with the symptom, not the cause

At 3 a.m., nobody knows the cause yet. They know the symptom: black picture, no audio, freeze, colour bars on air, alarm on the encoder. So organise runbooks by what the operator sees:

  • Picture frozen on main output
  • Audio missing on air
  • Satellite uplink alarm
  • Mains power failure

Each one begins with “restore air” steps, then moves to “diagnose and report” steps.

Put the decision points in writing

Good runbooks don’t remove judgement. They tell people where judgement is needed and give them a default:

If you are unsure whether to switch to backup, switch. A backup picture is always better than a frozen one.

Defaults like this are leadership. They decide in advance, on a calm day, what the team should do on a stressful night, and they take the fear out of acting.

Include who to call, and when

Every runbook ends with escalation:

  • Who to call (role first, then name and number).
  • When to call: immediately, after 10 minutes, or at start of business.
  • What to tell them: what happened, what you did, what’s on air now.

This protects both sides. Juniors know they are allowed to call. Seniors receive a clear summary instead of a confused one.

Keep it alive

Runbooks rot. Equipment changes, panels get relabelled, and a step that worked last year becomes wrong. Some habits that keep them useful:

  • Review after every incident. Did the runbook help? Where did people deviate, and why?
  • Have new staff test them. If a newcomer gets stuck, the runbook is wrong, not the newcomer.
  • Date every page and name an owner.
  • Keep them where the work is: printed at the desk and on a shared drive, in case the network is the thing that failed.

Why this is leadership

It’s tempting to see documentation as admin work that gets in the way of “real” engineering. I see it the other way around. A runbook spreads the knowledge of one experienced person to every shift. It lets juniors act confidently. It makes the team less dependent on any single hero.

Leadership isn’t only about being there when things go wrong. It’s about making sure the team is ready when you’re not.

If you want the wider context of how runbooks fit with handovers and checklists, read Running an MCR like a high-reliability operation.

Summary

  • A runbook guides action; it isn’t a manual.
  • Write for a tired, new person.
  • Start with the symptom, not the cause.
  • Put decision points and escalation contacts in writing.
  • Keep it alive after every incident.
