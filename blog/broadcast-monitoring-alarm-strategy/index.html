Monitoring and Alarm Strategy for Broadcast: Which Signals Matter, Cutting Alert Fatigue, and What to Put on the MCR Wall

A control room with hundreds of alarms isn't well monitored. It's noisy. How to decide which signals matter, design alarms people actually trust, and lay out a multiviewer wall that shows problems before viewers notice.

Abdullah Al Fahim23 Nov 2026 · 6 min read

  8. Summary

Walk into many Master Control Rooms and you’ll see the same thing: a wall of screens, an alarm panel with several red and yellow lights that have been on for weeks, and operators who have learned to ignore most of it.

That isn’t a failure of the people. It’s a failure of the monitoring design. When everything alarms, nothing alarms. In this post I’ll share a practical way to decide what to monitor, how to cut alert fatigue, and how to arrange the MCR wall so the right problem catches the right eye at the right moment.

Photo: Little League Softball World Series, CC0, via rawpixel.

Start with the question: what does the viewer experience?

Monitoring often grows device by device: every new box comes with its own SNMP traps, its own web page and its own beeping. The result reflects the equipment list, not the service.

Turn it around. Start from what viewers and advertisers care about:

  1. Is the channel on air?
  2. Is the picture right? Not black, not frozen, correct aspect ratio, no corruption.
  3. Is the audio right? Present, at the correct loudness, on the correct channels, in sync with the picture.
  4. Is the content right? Correct programme, correct commercial, correct logo, ticker and graphics.
  5. Is the transmission healthy? Encoder, multiplexer, modulator and uplink within normal limits, and the received signal looks right off-air.

Everything you monitor should connect to one of these questions. If a signal doesn’t, it probably belongs in a log, not on an alarm panel.

Three layers of monitoring

I find it useful to think in three layers:

1. Off-air confidence (the truth). Receive your own channel the way viewers do: from the satellite, from cable, from the OTT stream. If the off-air feed looks right, the viewer is fine, whatever the internal alarms say. If it looks wrong, nothing else matters until it’s fixed.

2. Signal quality along the chain. Black and freeze detection, silence detection, loudness measurement, video and audio presence, transport stream analysis (for example, the priority checks in ETSI TR 101 290), encoder status, modulator and HPA levels.

3. Infrastructure health. Power, UPS, temperatures, fans, disk space, network links, sync reference, server CPU. These matter, but they usually give you time: a rising temperature is a warning, not yet an outage.

Each layer needs a different kind of attention. Off-air problems need instant action. Infrastructure trends need daily review.

Classify every alarm

An alarm without a clear action is noise. For each alarm, decide:

Level Meaning Who reacts Example
Critical Viewers affected now or within seconds MCR operator, immediately Off-air black, silence on PGM, uplink loss
Major Redundancy lost or quality degraded Operator now, engineer informed Backup encoder down, main PSU failed
Minor Something needs attention this shift Shift engineer Disk space at 80%, fan warning
Info Record only Nobody, unless reviewing Login events, routine switching

A simple test: if an alarm can be ignored safely for a week, it isn’t critical, and it shouldn’t sound like one.

Cutting alert fatigue

Alert fatigue happens when people see so many alarms that they stop reacting to any of them. Practical ways to reduce it:

  • Tune thresholds. A black detector that fires on a deliberate fade to black between programmes teaches operators to ignore it. Use sensible durations (for example, several seconds of black, not one frame) and exclude known transitions where possible.
  • Suppress duplicates. One input failure can trigger alarms on every device downstream. Group them, or alarm on the root cause only.
  • Silence what’s being handled. Acknowledging an alarm should stop the noise but keep the alarm visible until it clears.
  • Remove “permanent” alarms. If an alarm has been red for weeks, either fix the cause, change the threshold or remove it. A permanently red light is training people to ignore red.
  • Separate sound from light. Use audible alarms only for critical events. Everything else can be visual.
  • Review alarms monthly. Which alarms fired most often? Which ones led to action? Delete or tune the rest.

The goal isn’t fewer alarms for its own sake. It’s making sure that when an alarm sounds, people trust it and act.

Designing the MCR wall

A multiviewer wall is a user interface. Good design principles apply.

Put the most important things in the most visible places. The programme output (PGM) and off-air return of each channel belong in the centre of the operator’s natural line of sight, larger than everything else.

Group by channel, then by chain. For each channel: PGM, off-air, preview, main and backup playout, and key incoming feeds. Keep the same order for every channel so operators don’t have to search.

Use overlays wisely. Audio meters, tally, source names, timecode and alarm borders are useful. Too many overlays turn every tile into clutter. Show what helps a decision.

Make the alarm state obvious. A red border on the affected tile is faster to understand than a red light on a separate panel. The eye goes straight to the problem.

Show time. A large clock synchronised to your station reference, and ideally the countdown to the next scheduled event (the next break, the next live programme).

Leave space for the unusual. Keep a few tiles free for live events, temporary feeds or a specific signal an engineer is watching during troubleshooting.

Test the wall from the operator’s chair. Labels that look readable on a design drawing can be unreadable from two metres away at 3 a.m.

Use trends, not just alarms

Some of the most useful monitoring isn’t alarms at all. It’s trends:

  • Equipment temperature slowly rising over weeks
  • Fans or power supplies with increasing warnings
  • Disk space shrinking on playout servers
  • An incoming feed that drops for a few seconds every evening

Trends feed preventive maintenance. They let you fix the cause on a quiet morning instead of during prime time. I’ll go deeper into that in the next post in this series.

Monitoring is also a people system

Finally, remember that monitoring is only useful when someone is looking and knows what to do:

  • Every critical alarm should link to a short procedure: first checks and first actions.
  • New operators need training on what’s normal, not just on what’s alarming.
  • The handover between shifts should include active alarms and anything being watched.

The best MCR walls I’ve seen aren’t the biggest. They’re the calmest: a few clear signals, trusted alarms, and operators who notice problems before the phone rings.

Summary

  • Start from what viewers see and hear, not the equipment list.
  • Monitor off-air, signal quality and infrastructure as three layers.
  • Classify every alarm: critical, major, minor or info.
  • Cut alert fatigue by tuning thresholds and removing permanent alarms.
  • Use trends for maintenance, and link every critical alarm to a procedure.
