Blameless Post-Mortems in a Broadcast Engineering Team

After an on-air fault, the easy question is 'who did this?'. The useful question is 'what let this happen?'. Here is how blameless reviews work in a broadcast team, and why they make channels more reliable, not less accountable.

Abdullah Al Fahim22 Nov 2026 · 4 min read

  2. When to hold one

When something goes wrong on air, there is a natural urge to find the person responsible. A wrong button, a missed step, a forgotten note at handover. Find them, correct them, move on.

It feels like accountability. In practice, it usually makes the next incident more likely. People who expect blame start hiding small mistakes, and small hidden mistakes are exactly what grow into big outages.

A blameless post-mortem takes a different approach. It assumes that people acted reasonably with what they knew at the time, and asks what in the system (the process, tools, information, timing) allowed the problem to happen.

Blameless doesn’t mean consequence-free

This is the most common misunderstanding. Blameless doesn’t mean “nobody is responsible” or “anything goes”. Deliberate negligence or repeated disregard for safety is still a management issue.

What blameless means is: in the review, we focus on causes, not culprits. The goal is learning, and people only share honestly when they don’t fear punishment for honest mistakes.

When to hold one

Not every glitch needs a meeting. A useful rule:

  • Any on-air impact viewers could notice.
  • Any near miss where only luck or a fast reaction saved air.
  • Any repeated small problem.

Near misses are especially valuable. They show you the weak spot before it costs anything.

The structure I use

Keep it short and focused. Within a few days of the incident, while memories are fresh:

1. Build the timeline

What happened, minute by minute? Use logs, automation records, monitoring alarms and the people involved. Write facts, not interpretations: “02:14 main encoder alarm”, not “02:14 operator ignored the alarm”.

2. Ask “what made sense at the time?”

For each decision, ask why it seemed right to the person making it. Maybe the alarm looked like a common false alarm. Maybe the runbook said something different. Maybe two alarms arrived together. This is where the real causes appear.

3. Find the contributing factors

There is rarely one root cause. Usually it’s several things lining up:

  • A missing step in a runbook
  • An unclear label on a panel
  • A handover that didn’t mention an unstable feed
  • An alarm that fires so often people stopped trusting it

4. Agree on actions, with owners

Each action should be specific, owned and dated: “Rename the two encoder inputs on the router panel so they can’t be confused. Owner: X. By Friday.” Avoid actions like “be more careful”. That’s not a fix. It’s a hope.

5. Share what you learned

A short written summary goes to the whole team, not just the people involved. Other shifts will meet the same weak spot one day.

Language matters

Small changes in wording change the whole meeting:

  • Say “what happened”, not “what went wrong with you”.
  • Say “the switch was pressed”, not “you pressed the wrong switch”.
  • Ask “what would have helped you see it sooner?”, not “why didn’t you notice?”

As the person running the review, your tone sets the safety level. If you stay curious, people stay honest.

Why it makes teams better

Over time, blameless reviews change a team:

  • People report near misses voluntarily, because reporting is valued.
  • Fixes go into systems (labels, alarms, runbooks, training) instead of depending on individuals trying harder.
  • Juniors learn faster, because they hear how experienced people think.
  • Trust grows between operators and engineers, who stop seeing each other as the problem.

Broadcast is a team sport played against the clock. A team that learns honestly from every fault gets steadily harder to knock off air.

Summary

  • Blameless means focusing on systems, not that nothing changes.
  • Hold a review for every on-air incident and serious near miss.
  • Go through the timeline, impact, causes, what went well and actions.
  • Use neutral language: what happened, not who failed.
  • Every action needs an owner and a due date.
