Running a Creative and Branding Team of Volunteers: What Worked

Five national skill development summits, thousands of students, and a branding team made entirely of volunteers. Here are the practical systems that let NUSDF's creative team ship consistent work on time.

During my time as Head of Branding & Communication at NUSDF, our creative team helped deliver five national skill development summits for more than 5,000 students. Every poster, social media post, banner, certificate and event screen came from volunteers: students juggling classes, exams and their own lives.

Big events with volunteer designers usually go one of two ways: everything is last-minute and inconsistent, or one person ends up doing it all. We tried hard to avoid both. These are the systems that made the biggest difference.

Photo: Free sticky notes office board, CC0, via rawpixel.

1. A brand kit before the first poster

The single most useful thing we built wasn’t a design. It was a shared brand kit:

  • Logo files in every format, with clear rules for light and dark backgrounds.
  • A small, fixed colour palette.
  • Two typefaces: one for headlines, one for body, with Bangla and English pairs.
  • Ready-made templates for the most common outputs: event posters, speaker cards, social posts, certificates.

A brand kit turns “design something for the summit” into “fill this template well”. New volunteers could contribute in their first week, and everything still looked like one organisation. I wrote more about building systems like this in Building a design system from one colour.

2. One request channel

Requests used to arrive everywhere: personal messages, group chats, comments in passing. Things got lost and some people got overloaded. We moved to one request form with a few required fields:

  • What is needed and where it will be used.
  • Text content, final and approved.
  • Deadline, and the real reason for it.
  • Who approves it.

The “final text” field alone saved hours. Most design delays aren’t design delays. They’re content arriving late or changing three times.

3. A visible board

Every request became a card on a simple board: Requested → In progress → Review → Approved → Published. Everyone could see who was working on what and what was waiting.

The board did two quiet jobs. It stopped me from becoming the bottleneck, and it made workload visible, so it was obvious when one designer had too much.

4. Pair seniors with juniors

Experienced designers were rare and busy. New volunteers were eager but unsure. We paired them: a junior took the first draft, a senior reviewed and coached. Juniors improved fast, seniors weren’t swamped, and we were never stuck when one person was unavailable.

5. Feedback with a structure

Unstructured feedback (“make it pop”, “something feels off”) frustrated everyone. We used a short order:

  1. Does it meet the brief? Right information, right size, right deadline.
  2. Is it on brand? Colours, fonts, logo use.
  3. Is it clear? Can someone understand it in three seconds?
  4. Then taste and polish.

Feedback in this order fixed the important problems first and kept personal taste from dominating. More on that in Giving and taking critique as a designer.

6. Deadlines with buffer, honestly explained

We set internal deadlines a few days before real ones, and we explained why: printing time, approval time, social media scheduling. Volunteers respected deadlines more when they understood them.

7. Credit on every published piece

Designers were credited in captions, in our internal updates and in end-of-event thank-yous. For a student building a portfolio, a public credit on a national event is worth a lot. It also made people care about quality, because their name was attached.

What I’d do differently

  • Start the brand kit even earlier, before the first event is announced.
  • Document recurring tasks sooner, so handover between committee years is smoother.
  • Protect exam periods more firmly in the calendar.

The takeaway

Volunteer creative teams don’t fail from lack of talent. They fail from lack of systems. A brand kit, one request channel, a visible board, paired mentoring and structured feedback let a group of busy students produce work that looked like it came from a professional studio. That’s what leadership in a creative team looks like: building the structure so the talent can show.

Summary

  • Create a brand kit before the first poster.
  • Use one request channel and one visible board.
  • Pair seniors with juniors.
  • Give structured feedback and honest deadlines with buffer.
  • Credit everyone on every published piece.
