RGB vs CMYK vs Pantone: Why Your Logo Looks Different on Screen and in Print

The bright red on your screen came back brown from the printer. Here is why RGB, CMYK and Pantone spot colours behave differently, and how to specify a brand colour so it survives every medium.

After reading this, you’ll know why colours change between screen and print, and how to specify a brand colour so it stays consistent.

Photo by paine_z on Pixabay (Pixabay Content License).

Why it matters

Almost every designer has had the “my red turned brown” moment. The logo looked perfect on screen, the printed banners arrived, and the colour was duller, darker or just different. Clients notice. Brand consistency depends on understanding that screens and printers make colour in completely different ways.

This builds on last week’s post, Colour profiles explained.

The idea explained

  • RGB (additive): screens emit red, green and blue light. Add all three at full strength and you get white. Screens can show very bright, saturated colours because they produce light.
  • CMYK (subtractive): printers lay down cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink in tiny dots. Ink absorbs light; paper reflects what’s left. Many bright screen colours, especially vivid reds, oranges, greens and blues, can’t be reached with four inks. They’re out of gamut.
  • Pantone spot colours: instead of mixing a colour from four inks on paper, the printer uses one ink pre-mixed to an exact formula. Spot colours can reach colours CMYK can’t (including metallics and fluorescents) and stay more consistent between print runs.

So the same “brand red” can be described three ways, and each description is a different physical process.

A real example

Imagine a brand red defined only as #E4002B on screen:

  • On screen (RGB): bright and clean.
  • Printed in CMYK after an automatic conversion: still red, but it can lose brightness and drift slightly towards brown or orange, depending on paper and press.
  • Printed with a matching Pantone spot ink: much closer to the intended colour, because the ink was mixed for it.

The fix isn’t to choose one system. It’s to define the colour in all of them and tell each supplier which one to use.

Try it yourself

Create a simple colour spec for one of your brand colours:

Use Value
Screen (web, apps, social) HEX and RGB
Four-colour print CMYK values (agreed with your printer or from the Pantone guide)
Spot print (signage, packaging, premium print) Pantone number, coated (C) and uncoated (U)
Video Rec.709 RGB (usually the same as screen values)

Then ask a printer for a proof of the CMYK version and compare it with your screen in daylight. You’ll quickly see which colours need adjusting.

Common mistakes

  • Designing print files in RGB and letting the printer’s RIP convert automatically, without a proof.
  • Choosing brand colours only on screen. Check early whether they can be printed.
  • Using one CMYK value for every paper. Coated and uncoated paper give different results.
  • Expecting a perfect match. Screens and paper will never be identical. The goal is consistent and approved, not identical.
  • Forgetting the black. Rich black (CMYK with extra C, M, Y) prints deeper than 100% K alone for large areas, but isn’t suitable for small text.

Summary

  • Screens add light (RGB). Printers subtract light with ink (CMYK).
  • Many bright screen colours can’t be reproduced with CMYK inks.
  • Pantone spot inks are pre-mixed for accuracy and reach colours CMYK can’t.
  • Define every brand colour in HEX/RGB, CMYK and Pantone.
  • Always proof printed colour, on the actual paper.
