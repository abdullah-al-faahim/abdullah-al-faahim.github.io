Broadcast-Safe Colour: What a TV Control Room Knows That Designers Don't

A neon lower-third that looks great in Figma can clip, smear or get rejected on air. A broadcast engineer's guide to legal range, Rec.709, colour bars and designing graphics that survive television.

After reading this, you’ll know how to design TV graphics that stay clean on air, and how to check them before they reach the control room.

Photo by This_is_Engineering on Pixabay (Pixabay Content License).

Why it matters

Designers make on-air graphics in tools built for screens: Figma, Photoshop, After Effects. Television has stricter rules. In the Master Control Room at Channel i, we watch every second of output on monitors and scopes. A graphic with colours outside the broadcast range doesn’t just look a bit different: it can clip (lose detail), smear after compression, or be flagged by quality control.

Few designers are taught this. It’s one of the places where my two jobs, designer and broadcast engineer, meet most directly.

The idea explained

Three ideas explain almost everything:

1. Rec.709 colours. HD television uses the BT.709 standard, which shares its colour primaries with sRGB. So far, so familiar. (See Colour profiles explained.)

2. Legal (limited) range. Computer graphics use the full 0–255 range for each channel. Broadcast video traditionally uses a limited range: in 8-bit video, black sits at code 16 and white at 235 (64 and 940 in 10-bit). Values outside that range are “illegal” or out of range.

3. Luma and chroma. Video stores brightness (luma) and colour difference (chroma) separately. Very saturated colours, especially bright reds, yellows and magentas, can push the combined signal beyond safe limits even if each RGB value looks fine.

Engineers check this with a waveform monitor (brightness) and a vectorscope (colour saturation and hue). Colour bars are the reference: if the bars sit at their targets, the chain is calibrated.

A real example

A common case: a lower-third with pure #FF0000 red background and pure white text.

  • Before: the red saturates the vectorscope beyond the target box, and pure white text sits at the absolute top of the range. After compression, the red edges can bleed into the text.
  • After: the red is pulled back slightly (lower saturation and brightness), and text is set to a light off-white. On a normal TV, viewers see the same design. On the scopes, it sits comfortably inside the limits.

Try it yourself

  1. Design in Rec.709/sRGB, not wide-gamut colour.
  2. Avoid pure maximum values for large areas: pure red, pure yellow, pure white backgrounds.
  3. Use slightly off-white text for large on-air text, and keep thin lines and fine text away from saturated edges.
  4. Check your export with scopes. DaVinci Resolve and Premiere Pro both have waveform and vectorscope views. Many editors also offer a “broadcast safe” or video-limiter effect.
  5. Ask your broadcaster for their delivery specification. It defines levels, loudness, formats and safe areas.
  6. Respect title-safe areas, so text isn’t cropped on some displays.

Common mistakes

  • Treating TV like a phone screen. Video has range limits and compression.
  • Relying on a limiter to fix everything. It can clip and change your colours unpredictably. Design within range from the start.
  • Thin, saturated details. Fine red lines and small coloured text break up after compression.
  • Mixing full- and limited-range exports. Wrong range flags make blacks grey or crush shadows.
  • Ignoring the delivery spec. Every channel has one; follow it.

Summary

  • TV uses Rec.709 colours with a limited legal range.
  • Very saturated or pure maximum colours can exceed broadcast limits.
  • Engineers check graphics with waveform monitors, vectorscopes and colour bars.
  • Design within range: soften pure colours and use off-white text.
  • Check scopes before delivery, and follow the broadcaster’s specification.
