Moving from SDI to IP Workflows (SMPTE ST 2110): Planning, Risks and a Phased Migration Roadmap for Bangladesh

Most Bangladeshi TV channels still run on SDI cables built a decade or more ago. Here is how a channel in Dhaka can plan a move to SMPTE ST 2110: what it changes, the local risks, and a phased roadmap that never puts the on-air signal at risk.

Walk into almost any TV station in Dhaka and you will find the same thing behind the racks: thick bundles of coaxial cable, BNC connectors and a large SDI router at the centre of everything. It is reliable, it is familiar, and the engineers who built it know every path by heart.

The rest of the broadcast world is slowly replacing that cable with network switches, fibre and software, mostly built on a family of standards called SMPTE ST 2110. The question for Bangladeshi channels is not whether IP arrives, but when, how fast and in what order, because our budgets, our power supply, our import process and our skills market are different from those of a broadcaster in London or Montreal.

I work in a Master Control Room at Channel i, so this post looks at the move from the inside: what changes, what can go wrong, and a phased roadmap that keeps the on-air signal safe the whole way through.

Where Bangladeshi broadcasting stands today

Bangladesh has around three dozen television channels on air, the state broadcaster BTV plus a large group of private satellite channels, most of them headquartered within a few kilometres of each other in Dhaka. Many of these facilities were designed between the mid-2000s and the mid-2010s, when the industry moved from tape to file-based workflows and from SD towards HD.

A typical Bangladeshi facility today looks like this:

  • An SDI core. HD-SDI (or 3G-SDI) routing, with some SD still in archives and older equipment.
  • File-based ingest and playout, driven by automation, with news and programmes on shared storage.
  • Satellite uplink for the main channel, including on the country’s own communications satellite launched in 2018, plus a teleport or backup path.
  • Many distribution routes at once: cable operators, DTH, IPTV, and a growing share of viewers on YouTube, Facebook and channel apps.
  • Field contribution over the internet, using bonded cellular units and streaming protocols, because district bureaus and live events rarely have dedicated fibre.

So in practice our channels are already half on IP at the edges. What is still SDI is the core: the router, the production switchers, the master control chain and the cables between them. That core is what ST 2110 replaces.

SDI and ST 2110 in one minute

SDI carries uncompressed video, embedded audio and data down one coaxial cable, point to point, with almost no delay. HD-SDI runs at about 1.5 Gb/s, 3G-SDI at about 3 Gb/s and 12G-SDI at about 12 Gb/s for UHD. It is simple and deterministic. Its weakness is that every new connection needs a physical cable and a router port, and every format upgrade tends to mean new hardware.

ST 2110 sends the same professional signals over a managed IP network, but splits them into separate streams (“essences”):

  • ST 2110-20: uncompressed video
  • ST 2110-30: uncompressed audio (based on AES67)
  • ST 2110-40: ancillary data such as captions and timecode
  • ST 2110-22: compressed video (for example JPEG XS) where bandwidth matters
  • ST 2110-21: rules for how evenly packets are sent

Around it sit three things every engineer has to learn:

  • Timing with PTP. Devices agree on time using the Precision Time Protocol, under the broadcast profile in SMPTE ST 2059. A GPS-locked grandmaster replaces the old reference generator. Timing stops being a cable and becomes a network service.
  • Redundancy with ST 2022-7. Each stream is sent twice over two separate networks (“red” and “blue”), and receivers rebuild a clean signal from whichever packets arrive. It is the IP version of the main and backup chains every MCR already runs, which I described in Inside a Master Control Room.
  • Control with AMWA NMOS. IS-04 lets devices announce themselves and IS-05 connects senders to receivers, so a control system can behave like the router panel operators already know.

Why a Bangladeshi channel would move at all

IP should never be adopted because it is fashionable. In our market the real drivers are:

  1. End-of-life SDI cores. Routers, switchers and frame-sync cards bought around 2010 to 2015 are reaching the end of vendor support. When a core has to be replaced anyway, buying IP-ready equipment avoids paying twice.
  2. HD everywhere, and UHD later. Upgrading formats on IP is mostly about bandwidth and licences, not rewiring the building.
  3. Remote and distributed production. Cricket, elections, Ekushey February, Eid programming and big talk shows increasingly mix studios, outside locations and remote guests. IP makes it easier to share sources between studios, control rooms and even other buildings.
  4. Digital-first distribution. When a large part of the audience watches on YouTube, Facebook and apps, the facility needs to feed many outputs at once. Software-based playout and graphics fit naturally on an IP plant.
  5. A local skills advantage. Bangladesh has a deep pool of networking talent from its ISPs, telecom operators and IT sector. Routing, switching and multicast are skills we can hire and grow locally.

Planning: what to settle before buying anything

1. Write down why. One page: what problem the move solves, what success looks like, and what must never happen (usually: never lose the on-air signal).

2. Inventory the plant. List every SDI source, destination, format, frame rate and audio map. Mark what is end-of-life, what is new and what can stay SDI for years with a gateway.

3. Decide the architecture early. Two common network designs exist: a large central switch, or a spine-and-leaf fabric that scales by adding switches. For most Bangladeshi channels, a modest spine-leaf design with fully separate red and blue networks is a sensible default.

4. Do the bandwidth sums. One uncompressed 1080p50 or 1080i stream is roughly 1.5 to 3 Gb/s. Multiply by sources, then add headroom, then double it for 2022-7 redundancy. Link speeds, switch buffers and optics follow from this, not from a brochure.

5. Choose standards over brands. Insist on ST 2110, ST 2059 PTP, ST 2022-7 and NMOS IS-04/IS-05, and ask vendors for proof of interoperability, such as participation in the JT-NM Tested programme. Avoid any design that only works with one manufacturer’s controller.

6. Count the full cost, in taka. Budget for switches, optics, PTP grandmasters, gateways, a broadcast controller, monitoring, training, spares held locally, UPS and cooling, not just the headline devices. Most equipment is priced in US dollars and imported, so exchange-rate movement and import timelines belong in the plan from day one.

7. Plan for people. Who will run the network at 3 a.m.? Broadcast and IT staff need to become one team, with shared on-call rules and the same checklists and handovers the MCR already depends on.

The risks, and how to manage them

Risk What it looks like in Bangladesh How to reduce it
Timing (PTP) failure Lip-sync drift, devices that won’t lock, glitches nobody can explain Two GPS-locked grandmasters, PTP-aware switches, timing alarms in the MCR, a simple PTP runbook
Network misconfiguration A multicast flood or wrong IGMP setting takes down many feeds at once Change control, a test lab, configuration backups, strict separation of red and blue networks
Interoperability gaps A camera, switcher and controller from three vendors don’t agree Standards-based tenders, JT-NM-tested kit, a proof-of-concept before the main order
Skills gap SDI veterans and IT staff speak different languages Joint training, a small lab to practise on, pair broadcast and network engineers
Import and currency delays Equipment held up by letters of credit, dollar shortages or customs; prices rise mid-project Order critical items early, phase purchases, keep local spares, fix prices where possible
Power and heat Load-shedding, generator switchover glitches, hot and humid equipment rooms Double-conversion UPS on all network gear, dual power supplies, proper cooling and dust control
Cyber security A networked plant can be reached from places a coaxial cable never could Segmented networks, no direct internet on media networks, access control, logging
On-air disruption during cutover A migration step goes wrong live Never cut over the main chain first; keep SDI fallback paths and rehearse rollback

A phased roadmap that keeps the channel on air

The safest migrations do not replace everything at once. They grow IP from the edges inward, with a working SDI fallback at every step. The durations below are typical for a mid-sized channel and will vary with budget and import timelines.

Phase 0: Readiness (3 to 6 months)

  • Train a core team in multicast, IGMP, PTP and ST 2110 basics.
  • Build a small lab: two switches, a PTP grandmaster, a few gateways and a monitoring tool.
  • Complete the inventory, the architecture and the risk register.
  • Exit when: the team can build, break and fix a small 2110 network in the lab.

Phase 1: IP at the edges (6 to 12 months)

  • Move audio first with AES67-compatible equipment. It teaches the same ideas (PTP, multicast, discovery) at a fraction of the bandwidth and risk.
  • Standardise field contribution on streaming protocols such as SRT for remote bureaus and events.
  • Use IP for non-critical paths such as monitoring feeds and multiviewers.
  • Exit when: IP audio and contribution run daily without incidents.

Phase 2: The first IP island (12 to 18 months)

  • Build one new studio or production control room natively on ST 2110, with gateways to the existing SDI core.
  • Install red and blue networks, redundant PTP, an NMOS-based broadcast controller and network monitoring.
  • Operate the island under real production pressure while the SDI core stays untouched.
  • Exit when: the island has run real shows for months with documented fault handling.

Phase 3: Core and master control (18 to 30 months)

  • Replace the SDI router with the IP fabric, moving sources across in planned windows.
  • Migrate master control and playout last, with an SDI or tested backup chain kept ready for rollback.
  • Update every MCR procedure, alarm and handover note for the new plant.
  • Exit when: the main and backup chains both run on IP and failover has been rehearsed.

Phase 4: Optimise and grow (ongoing)

  • Move more processing into software: graphics, multiviewers, playout.
  • Extend remote production between studios, bureaus and venues.
  • Prepare for UHD or higher frame rates where the business case exists.

Many Bangladeshi channels will reasonably stay hybrid for years: SDI where it still works well, IP where it clearly helps, and gateways in between. That is not failing to modernise. It is good engineering under real constraints.

What the rest of the world teaches us

We don’t have to learn every lesson ourselves:

  • BBC Cymru Wales opened its Central Square headquarters in Cardiff in 2020, widely cited as one of the first large broadcast centres built around ST 2110. Its main lesson for others has been how much effort goes into integration, timing and testing compared with simply buying equipment.
  • CBC/Radio-Canada moved into its new Montreal headquarters around the same time, also designed as a large IP facility, showing that a public broadcaster can migrate a whole building while staying on air.
  • The EBU (European Broadcasting Union) publishes guidance for broadcasters, including its Technology Pyramid for Media Nodes (EBU Tech 3371), which helps buyers specify the minimum capabilities IP equipment should have.
  • The JT-NM Tested programme checks how well products from different vendors follow ST 2110, PTP and NMOS. Its reports are a useful filter before writing a tender.
  • AMWA maintains the NMOS specifications as open, free documents, so any engineer in Dhaka can read exactly what a controller is supposed to do.

The common thread is consistent: successful broadcasters treated IP migration as a change in people and processes as much as in technology, and they kept a safe path to air throughout.

Where a Bangladeshi engineer should start

  1. Learn networking properly. Multicast and IGMP are the heart of ST 2110. Our ISP and telecom sector is full of people who can teach this.
  2. Understand PTP. Most IP plant problems eventually lead back to timing.
  3. Practise with audio. AES67 equipment is affordable and teaches the same concepts.
  4. Read the standards summaries. SMPTE ST 2110 parts, ST 2059 and NMOS IS-04/IS-05. Know what each does.
  5. Bring the MCR mindset with you. Signal paths, redundancy, checklists and rehearsed failover matter even more on IP.

The coaxial cable on the desk is going away, slowly. The job isn’t. It is still about getting a clean picture from one place to another, on time, without the viewer ever noticing, whether that viewer is in Dhaka, in a district town, or watching a Bangladeshi channel on a phone halfway around the world.

Summary

  • Most Bangladeshi channels still run on SDI, and that’s fine for now.
  • ST 2110 sends video, audio and data as separate IP streams.
  • Settle timing, networking and skills before buying.
  • Move in phases: edges first, the on-air core last.
  • Learn from others and start building IP skills now.
